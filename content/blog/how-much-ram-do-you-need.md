Every PC listing throws a RAM number at you like it's supposed to mean something. 8GB, 16GB, 32GB, 64GB. Laptop ads make 8GB sound plenty. Gaming PC builds insist you need 32GB minimum. Meanwhile you're just trying to figure out why your computer slows to a crawl when you have more than six Chrome tabs open.

Here's the straight answer.

## What RAM Actually Does

RAM is your computer's short-term memory. It's where your PC keeps everything that's currently running — your browser, your tabs, your music, your open documents. The more RAM you have, the more your PC can juggle at once without breaking a sweat.

It's completely separate from your storage. Your SSD or hard drive is where files live permanently. RAM is where they live *right now*, while you're using them.

## The Kitchen Counter Analogy

Think of your PC like a kitchen.

Your hard drive or SSD is the fridge and pantry — that's where everything is stored. RAM is your counter space, where you actually prep and work. A tiny counter means you can only have a few ingredients out at a time. The moment you want to cook something with 20 ingredients, you're constantly going back to the fridge, putting things away, grabbing new things, losing your rhythm.

More counter space means everything you need is just *there*. You move faster, you think less about logistics, and nothing grinds to a halt because you ran out of room.

When your PC slows down under load — that's a full counter. It's shuffling things around trying to make space, and that takes time.

## So How Much Do You Actually Need?

**8GB — You'll get by, but you'll feel it**

8GB was the comfortable baseline for years. In 2026 it's starting to show its age. Chrome alone can eat through 4–5GB with a handful of tabs open. Add Spotify, a PDF, and a Discord call and you're already pushing the limit. It works, but you'll notice the hesitation.

If you're buying something new with 8GB, think twice. If you already have it and your PC feels sluggish, this might be exactly why.

**16GB — The sweet spot for most people**

This is the answer for the overwhelming majority of people. Web browsing, Office apps, video calls, light photo editing, casual gaming — 16GB handles all of it without blinking. You can have 30 tabs open, music playing, and a spreadsheet going and not think about it once.

If someone asks me what to get, 16GB is what I say without hesitation.

**32GB — For people who push their machines**

You're looking at 32GB if you're doing serious gaming alongside streaming, video editing in Premiere or DaVinci Resolve, running virtual machines, or working with large datasets. It's not overkill for these use cases — it's appropriate.

It's overkill if you're mostly browsing and watching YouTube. Be honest with yourself about what you actually do.

**64GB and beyond — You know if you need it**

3D rendering, large-scale video production, professional software development environments running multiple containers at once. If you're at this level you're not reading a "how much RAM do I need" article — you already know.

## Does the Speed of RAM Matter?

Yes, but probably less than the marketing suggests. RAM comes in different speeds — DDR4, DDR5, with various MHz ratings. For most everyday tasks the difference between mid-range and high-end RAM speeds is barely noticeable in real use.

What matters more: **getting enough of it first**. A fast 8GB kit will lose to a slower 16GB kit in almost every real-world scenario. Get the right amount before worrying about speed.

## Can You Upgrade Your RAM Later?

Usually yes, and it's one of the easier upgrades you can do. Desktop PCs almost always have open RAM slots. Laptops are trickier — some have RAM soldered directly to the motherboard (meaning you can't upgrade it at all), while others have accessible slots.

If you're buying a laptop and you're not sure, look it up before you buy. It's a five-second search that could save you a lot of frustration later.

## The Chrome Problem

Quick side note because it comes up constantly: Chrome is genuinely a RAM hog. If you're on 8GB and feeling the squeeze, switching to Firefox or even Edge will actually free up a noticeable amount of memory. It's not a fix for not having enough RAM — but it buys you breathing room.
